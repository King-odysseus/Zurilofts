# What to do if personal data is lost, leaked or seen by the wrong person

Version: 2026-11-05. Owner: BeneAlpha Consulting Limited (trading as ZuriLofts).
Read this once now, so you are not reading it for the first time during an incident.

This is operating guidance, not legal advice. The 72-hour rule below comes from
Kenyan data protection guidance read on 2026-11-05 and has not been confirmed by a
lawyer. Ask your advocate to confirm it, and whether the company must register
with the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC), before relying on it.

## What counts

Any time personal data is lost, stolen, sent to the wrong person, shown to someone
who should not see it, or changed without permission. Examples: a guest list
emailed to the wrong address, a laptop with exports stolen, an admin account
taken over, identity documents visible in a link anyone can open, a supplier
telling us they were hacked.

If you are not sure, declare it. A declared incident that turns out to be nothing
costs ten minutes. A real incident nobody declared costs far more.

## The one rule that matters: the clock

The 72 hours start when anyone at the company first knew, or reasonably should have
known. It does not start when the investigation ends or when a manager hears.
Write down the real time. The register shows a countdown from it.

## Steps

**1. Within the first hour**
- Whoever finds it tells an admin straight away, by phone, not only by message.
- An admin opens **Admin, Data incidents, Declare an incident** and fills it in.
  The time the company first knew is the most important field. Be exact.
- Do not copy the leaked data into the register. Describe it and give a count.
- Stop the harm if you can: remove access, change passwords, take a link down,
  ask the wrong recipient to delete the message and confirm in writing.

**2. Within the first day**
- Work out which kinds of personal data were involved, how many people, and what
  could happen to them (fraud, embarrassment, safety). Edit the incident to add it.
- Write down what has been done to contain it.
- Decide, with the owner and your advocate, whether the people affected face a real
  risk. If yes, the regulator must be told. If you decide no, close the incident
  with a note that says why. That note is your evidence.

**3. Before 72 hours are up**
- Open the incident and press **Copy notice**. Read it. Fix anything wrong by
  editing the incident, then copy it again.
- Send it to the ODPC. The Office publishes how to report on its website
  (odpc.go.ke). Use the contact route it gives; do not guess an address.
- Straight after sending, press **Record that the regulator was told**. Enter the
  real time you sent it and the Office's reference if it gave one. This can only be
  done once, so check the time first.
- If you miss 72 hours, still send it and record it. The register will ask for the
  reason for the delay, because the regulator will.

**4. Tell the people affected**
- If there is a real risk to them, tell them in plain words: what happened, what
  data, what you have done, what they should do (for example change a password),
  and who to contact. The app does not send these messages for you; send them by
  email or phone yourselves.
- Then press **Record that they were told**.

**5. Close it**
- When it is contained and everyone who needed telling has been told, close the
  incident and say what was learned or changed. A closed incident cannot be edited.

## What the register records, and what it does not

It records who declared and updated each incident (in the governance log), the
time the company became aware, the times each notice was sent, and your notes.
It never deletes anything. It does not email anyone, and it does not store the
leaked data itself.

## Suppliers

If a supplier that holds our data (for example Paystack, Cloudinary or Railway)
tells us it was breached, the clock starts when we are told. ODPC guidance, as
read on 2026-11-05, expects a processor to tell the controller within 48 hours.
Check each supplier contract says so.
